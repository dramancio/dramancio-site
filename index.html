About my research

My research sits at the intersection of network science, natural language processing and machine learning. I am interested in complex systems whose behaviour emerges from the relationships between their parts, such as texts, the scientific literature and information flows, and I model them as networks to reveal patterns that traditional statistics often misses.

Since my PhD, I have studied language through networks, using the structure of word co-occurrence and semantic networks to investigate authorship, style, meaning and text quality. More recently, I have combined these representations with word embeddings and large language models. In parallel, I work on the science of science: how citations shape scientific impact, why papers are cited, how impact can be predicted, and how research is assessed and funded. I also develop and compare machine learning methods for graphs and data analysis; our comparative studies of clustering and classification methods and our tutorial on principal component analysis are widely used references.

New publication Journal of Informetrics · 2026

Not all citations are equal: teaching AI to read why scientists cite

Citations are the currency of science. They show how ideas spread, and they feed the indicators used to evaluate papers, researchers and institutions. But a citation can mean very different things: a paper may be cited as background, because its method was used, or to compare results. Counting every citation the same way hides that difference.

We built a compact AI model, based on generative adversarial networks (GANs), that reads the sentence around a citation and identifies its intent. It matches state-of-the-art accuracy with far fewer parameters, so it is cheaper to run at scale. Applying it to a large network of arXiv papers, we found that which papers look most central changes significantly depending on which kinds of citations are counted, especially for papers that bridge different fields.

Why it matters. Rankings of papers and researchers often rely on raw citation counts. This work shows that those rankings depend on why papers are cited, and offers an efficient tool to take citation intent into account: a step toward fairer, more meaningful research evaluation.

Original article D. A. Bezerra, F. N. Silva, D. R. Amancio “Leveraging GANs for citation intent classification and its impact on citation network analysis.” Journal of Informetrics 20(2), 101791 (2026).
